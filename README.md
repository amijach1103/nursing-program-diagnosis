# Diagnosing a nursing program against new competency standards

A client engagement I led in 2021 and 2022 at a research and development lab: a university nursing school's bachelor's and master's programs mapped against the American Association of Colleges of Nursing's new competency standards, then a workshop series to help faculty act on the results.

**About 80** courses analyzed in depth · **10** domains, **45** competencies · **a kickoff and 6** workshops

## Context

The American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) asked nursing schools to move to a competency-based model. A prominent nursing school came to us to start that process, and I led the first engagement: diagnose where the programs stood, and kick off the change with faculty and leadership. We talked with students, faculty and leadership to understand the student experience.

## How we diagnosed the programs

**The courses, mapped.** I wrote the program analysis reports for both programs. For each course, we reviewed the syllabus, schedules, sample assessments and the activities in the LMS. Each objective and its assessments were mapped to the domain, competency and sub-competency of the new standards it addressed. The reports analyzed about 80 courses in depth and tagged around 500 learning objectives. Courses taught outside the nursing school were listed but not analyzed.

**Coverage, four ways.** The reports show how often each domain is addressed, which competencies in each domain show up, how they're spread across semesters or tracks, and whether the level fits the program. The standards label sub-competencies as entry level or advanced, so we could check that a bachelor's program wasn't leaning on graduate-level ones.

**Activities, against the standards' own definition.** We split the standards' definition of active learning into six yes/no questions and asked them of every course we analyzed in depth.

**Recommendations.** Among them: match the verb in each objective to the level of competency the assessment asks for, and build in the diversity, equity and inclusion competencies the standards require. The report also suggested an assessment template, pre-assessments at the start of a semester, a common course home page and spaces for faculty to talk about student support.

## The workshop series

I designed and ran the series with a steering committee of administrators and faculty, over seven months. It was a kickoff and six workshops: one for leaders, one for faculty, then assessment design, curriculum mapping, active learning strategies and program evaluation. The workshops were virtual, and I owned the schedule and the communications with faculty. Most workshop plans used breakout rooms, by program, by semester or track, or by topic.

Some faculty were very worried about their lack of time, so some sessions made room for them to talk about it, and we adjusted.

For the later workshops, I packaged the recordings with worksheets and guides, and ran office hours so faculty leads could support their colleagues.

## What happened

In the follow-up, the checklists became a resource sent to all faculty, the active learning workshop was opened to the whole faculty, and the program analysis was called a good starting point. We took three things into the next engagement: a summary at the top of the report, shorter workshops and a stronger pre-assessment. Change in faculty practice after the engagement wasn't measured.

## Design decisions

- **Use the standards' own structure.** Results follow the standards' domains, competencies and sub-competencies.
- **Say what we couldn't see.** We judged what was visible in the course materials, and the report says so: where evaluation criteria weren't clear, it allows they might be clear in class.
- **The report feeds the workshops.** The analysis was meant as a baseline, with its examples used to model changes with faculty. The leaders' workshop agenda walked through it in order: the current map, what's missing, progression, then a plan.
